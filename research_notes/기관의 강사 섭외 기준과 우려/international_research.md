# How organizations select external trainers, speakers and training providers, and what risks they fear (international evidence)

Method note: This is a web-search pass of about 20 tool calls. The session's network policy blocked full-text fetches from trainingindustry.com, nber.org, speakers.ca and uni-muenchen.de, so many findings come from search-engine abstracts and snippets, not full texts. Where a figure comes only from a snippet or secondary summary, it is flagged. Check them against the originals before quoting any number publicly.

## Q1. What academic studies say about criteria for selecting external trainers/providers, and trainer characteristics in the training-transfer literature

### Takeaway
The mainstream transfer models (Baldwin & Ford 1988; Burke & Hutchins 2007; Grossman & Salas 2011) group transfer factors into trainee characteristics, training design/delivery and work environment. The trainer was not a separate factor until Burke & Hutchins (2008) proposed adding one. Small experiments show that trainer delivery (expressiveness plus organization) and trainer reputation affect learning outcomes, and reputation matters most for less engaged learners. Firm-level studies of provider selection describe high quality uncertainty: buyers lean on networks, intuition and past experience, and about 28% of managers in one study reported bad experiences with external providers.

### Cited Findings
**Transfer frameworks (where the trainer sits)**
- Grossman & Salas (2011), "The Transfer of Training: What Really Matters," *International Journal of Training and Development* 15(2):103–120. Uses Baldwin & Ford's model and groups factors into trainee characteristics (cognitive ability, self-efficacy, motivation, perceived utility), training design (behavioural modelling, error management, realistic environments) and work environment (climate, support, opportunity to perform, follow-up). Work-environment factors showed the strongest and most consistent relationships with transfer. The review prioritizes factors and does not try to list them all. — [DOI 10.1111/j.1468-2419.2011.00373.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2419.2011.00373.x); [full-text PDF](https://ftq.qc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2019/04/Grossman_et_al-2011-International_Journal_of_Training_and_Development.pdf)
- Burke & Hutchins (2007), "Training Transfer: An Integrative Literature Review," *Human Resource Development Review* 6(3):263–296. Organizes transfer factors into learner characteristics, intervention design and delivery, and work-environment influences. The trainer is not a separate category. — [DOI 10.1177/1534484307303035](https://doi.org/10.1177/1534484307303035)
- Burke & Hutchins (2008), "A Study of Best Practices in Training Transfer and Proposed Model of Transfer," *HRDQ* 19(2):107–128. Data came from training professionals in one southern U.S. ASTD chapter (sample size not visible in the snippets). The best transfer practices sit in the work context and in design and delivery, happen during or after training, and involve trainers and supervisors. The authors proposed adding "trainers" to the transfer model. Practitioners named several trainer characteristics as relevant to transfer, but the specific list was not visible in accessible snippets. — [DOI 10.1002/hrdq.1230](https://doi.org/10.1002/hrdq.1230); [secondary summary (Konstanz)](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/e23add50-9eb7-4aa1-91ca-4e5759924605/content)
- Baldwin & Ford (1988), "Transfer of Training: A Review and Directions for Future Research," *Personnel Psychology* 41(1):63–105. This is the original input–process–output transfer model (trainee characteristics, training design, work environment) that the reviews above build on. — [DOI 10.1111/j.1744-6570.1988.tb00632.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1744-6570.1988.tb00632.x). Citation details are from the researcher's background knowledge; the model's use is confirmed via Grossman & Salas above.

**Trainer delivery and reputation (experimental evidence)**
- Towler & Dipboye (2001), *Journal of Applied Psychology* 86(4):664–673. Lab experiment, N = 135. Lectures varied in trainer expressiveness and organization. Recall was highest when the lecture was both expressive and well organized. For problem solving, highly mastery-oriented trainees did worst after an organized but inexpressive lecture. — [DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.86.4.664](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.86.4.664); [abstract summary](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2025708141). The summary was truncated, so verify details.
- Towler & Dipboye (2006), "Effects of trainer reputation and trainees' need for cognition on training outcomes," *Journal of Psychology*. N = 75. Before a videotaped lecture, participants read that the trainer was effective, ineffective, or got no information. Trainees low in need for cognition were influenced by the trainer's reputation and high-need-for-cognition trainees were not. Self-efficacy mediated the effect on declarative knowledge. — [UCF STARS record](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib2000/6655); [record](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/900964645)
- Kodwani & Kodwani (2021), *Evidence-based HRM*. Survey of 251 managers at a large Indian firm, before and after training. Trainer and training reputation affected pre-training motivation, which related to training effectiveness. The authors note possible method bias. — [DOI 10.1108/EBHRM-05-2020-0070](https://www.doi.org/10.1108/EBHRM-05-2020-0070)
- A University of Oslo master's thesis studied 72 participants at Norsk Hydro. Training reputation was significantly correlated with transfer. This is about the program's reputation, not the individual trainer's. — [DUO record](https://duo.uio.no/handle/10852/55281)
- A *Safety Science* (2017) study of occupational-safety trainers found they felt responsible for training results but had little control over transfer. — [ISCTE record](https://ciencia.iscte-iul.pt/publications/exploring-ohs-trainers-role-in-the-transfer-of-training/33136?lang=en)

**Provider/vendor selection studies**
- Abraham, Arpagaus & Höglinger (c. 2008, conference paper, Venice) studied how managers search for external training providers and treated it as a market with quality uncertainty. 28% of respondents reported having had "bad experiences" with external training providers. The full text could not be fetched, so sample size, country and the network/recommendation findings are not verified here. — [PDF (uni-muenchen)](https://www.en.soziologie.uni-muenchen.de/venedig/venice08/hoeglinger_viu08.pdf)
- A 2011 *International Journal of Information Technology & Decision Making* paper argues that HR-led selection of training providers lacks measurable criteria, because most decisions rest on intuitive experience and subjective market knowledge. It proposes a formal multi-criteria model. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wsi/ijitdm/v10y2011i01ns0219622011004269.html)
- A 2020 *European Proceedings* paper builds a provider-selection algorithm based on ISO 9001 requirements, with a descriptive model of provider criteria and trainer qualifications. — [DOI 10.15405/epsbs.2020.03.123](https://www.europeanproceedings.com/pdf/article/10.15405/epsbs.2020.03.123)

### Inferences
- Trainer reputation works as a pre-training signal that raises motivation and self-efficacy, especially among less engaged learners. So credible trainer profiles (track record, reviews) may affect outcomes as well as purchase decisions.
- The academic transfer literature says the work environment (manager support, chance to apply) predicts transfer more strongly than the trainer. Buyers who judge a provider only on the trainer may misattribute failures. A provider that offers pre- and post-session support for transfer covers a factor the trainer alone cannot.
- Formal studies find that buyers choose providers by intuition and networks rather than structured criteria. That is a gap a structured matching or verification process could fill.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found, in HRDQ, HRDI, JEIT or IJTD, that gives a ranked, quantitative list of criteria buyers use to pick an individual external trainer. Searches returned frameworks and practitioner content instead.
- The specific trainer characteristics in Burke & Hutchins (2008) could not be read from accessible text.
- A direct study of "trainer credibility" (as distinct from reputation) and transfer in HRDQ was not found in this pass.
- The full text of the Höglinger et al. paper (sample, network-search percentages) was blocked by network policy.

## Q2. What industry surveys report about buyer priorities when choosing training providers and why engagements fail

### Takeaway
Industry sources consistently name the same shortlist: confidence the provider will deliver (trust), customization, industry expertise and reputation, quality of resources, cultural fit, cost, and proven track record. The available sources suggest price is not the dominant factor. Hard, recent percentages from ATD, CIPD, Brandon Hall or Gartner on provider-selection criteria could not be retrieved in this pass. Reported failure causes are mostly about process (delayed SME reviews, poor fit) rather than trainer quality.

### Cited Findings
- Training Industry, Inc. (Training Outsourcing special edition, 2015). When companies solicit proposals, the down-selection criteria generally include cost, trust in the provider to deliver, quality of resources, industry expertise and reputation, culture fit, and ability to customize. 61% of organizations prefer to outsource to specialized learning providers rather than generalists. — [Training Industry Magazine 2015, p. 28](https://nxtbook.com/nxtbooks/trainingindustry/tiq_2015outsourcing/index.php?startid=28)
- Training Industry research on sales training. Buyers use four standards when selecting a vendor: confidence in delivery, customization ability, industry expertise and cultural fit. Practitioners added ease of working together and a proven track record. Year and sample size were not visible because the page was blocked. — [Training Industry blog](https://trainingindustry.com/blog/sales/vendor-selection-tips-for-corporate-sales-training/)
- Training Industry outsourcing guidance recommends setting criteria before sourcing (cultural fit, company and team experience, financial stability, regulatory compliance) and weighting them, for example cost, delivery, quality and experience. It warns that project schedules are often sidetracked by delays in material reviews by internal SMEs. — [Training Industry article](https://trainingindustry.com/articles/learning-services-and-outsourcing/how-to-select-a-training-outsourcing-company/)
- A Training Magazine reader survey, reported by Personnel Today, was sponsored by a venue operator and is undated (likely mid-2000s). Three years earlier, 73% had said they selected a supplier purely on price. In the later survey, 89% said price was not a primary concern. This is an old, secondary and sponsor-linked source. — [Personnel Today](https://www.personneltoday.com/?p=61600)
- InfoPro Learning, a provider (vendor-authored, so treat as marketing-adjacent), reports that cost-effectiveness, trust, quality of resources and industry expertise ranked highest when organizations chose providers. — [InfoPro Learning PDF (2024)](https://www.infoprolearning.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Partnering-for-Success-External-Training-Solution-Providers_DeliveringReal-Results.pdf)
- A UK trade survey of installers (sector-specific): respondents would pay more for trainers with real industry experience, and reputation came second at 40%. — [PHPI Online](https://phpionline.co.uk/?p=6368)
- Personnel Today lists warning signs of a poor provider, such as no proven track record, and recommends re-tendering contracts every one to two years. — [Personnel Today](https://www.personneltoday.com/?p=43555)
- TrainingZone: buyers' criteria range from price, to past industry experience, to a provider's client list. — [TrainingZone](https://trainingzone.co.uk/?p=18642)
- Brandon Hall Group's 2017 Learning Technology Study (about technology vendors, not trainers) found that organizations without a formal selection team were 60% more likely to say their selection process was ineffective. — [Brandon Hall "Selecting the Right Learning Technology Vendor"](https://brandonhall.com/?p=27554)
- Market size, Training Magazine 2024 Training Industry Report. U.S. training expenditure fell 3.7% to $98B in 2024. Spending on outside products and services rose about 23% to $12.4B (up from $10.1B in 2023), per a secondary summary. In-house training payroll fell from $63B to $60.6B. — [Training Magazine 2024 Report](https://trainingmag.com/2024-training-industry-report/); [secondary summary](https://learnexperts.ai/?p=5058)

### Inferences
- In these sources, "trust in delivery" or "confidence to deliver" comes first or near the top. That points to delivery risk as buyers' main worry. Verified track record, references and outcome data would answer it directly.
- Outside spending rose while in-house payroll fell in 2024. That suggests rising demand for external providers, but it is a single year's figures from a secondary summary.
- Price matters, but the evidence suggests buyers do not choose on price alone. Customization and fit matter as much.

### Gaps
- No retrievable ATD, CIPD, SHRM or Gartner survey with percentages on criteria for selecting external trainers or providers. CIPD's Learning at Work surveys may cover use of external providers but were not reached.
- No quantitative industry data on why provider engagements fail (for example, the share failing for poor customization versus a trainer no-show). Only qualitative process causes were found.
- Brandon Hall's sales-training-provider survey names "important factors in selecting a sales training provider" as an objective, but the results were not accessible.

## Q3. What event planners and speaker bureaus report about speaker-booking risks and vetting practices

### Takeaway
Bureau and trade sources describe the same set of risks: wrong fit with the audience, generic or uncustomized content, last-minute cancellation (especially by big names), and controversial or off-brand remarks. They also describe the same mitigations: a briefing call before the event, checks on how deeply the speaker customizes, rehearsals, named backups and cancellation clauses in contracts. Almost all of this comes from bureaus with a commercial interest. No independent survey quantifying speaker no-show or controversy rates was found.

### Cited Findings
- A survey cited by a speakers bureau (survey sponsor and sample not named in the snippet) found the top challenges in booking a keynote speaker are finding the right fit, staying on budget, evaluating speakers with confidence, and getting the right support. — [Speakers.ca, July 2026](https://www.speakers.ca/2026/07/the-biggest-challenges-event-planners-face-when-booking-a-speaker-and-how-to-solve-them/). The page was blocked, so methodology is unverified.
- Worldwide Speakers Group (WWSG): one of the most common mistakes by DIY planners is having no plan B if the speaker cancels at the last minute. A conference call before the event lets the speaker hear the event objectives and the audience's size and make-up firsthand, and tailor content. WWSG almost always runs a pre-event call with both host and speaker. — [WWSG "4 critical mistakes"](https://info.wwsg.com/blog/4-critical-mistakes-event-planners-make-when-booking-a-professional-speaker); [WWSG 7 steps](https://wwsg.com/blog/7-steps-for-speaker-experience-and-event-success); [WWSG risk management](https://info.wwsg.com/blog/risk-management-how-the-wwsg-events-team-handles-issues-and-obstacles)
- All American Speakers: many planners sign contracts 4–6 weeks before the event, while meaningful customization typically needs 60–90 days. They advise evaluating how deeply a speaker customizes before booking. — [All American Speakers](https://www.allamericanspeakers.com/blog/speaker-customization-evaluating-depth-before-booking/)
- Executive Speakers Bureau quotes Peter Sheahan: use the briefing call to weed out speakers who don't plan to customize. — [Executive Speakers briefing-call guide](https://executivespeakers.com/blogdetails/event-planners-guide-to-the-keynote-speaker-briefing-call)
- Dame Lionel Network: hold a well-planned pre-event call about 3–4 weeks before the program. Speakers want a concise overview of what the event is, who the audience is, and what the client wants from their remarks. — [Damelio Network](https://blog.damelionetwork.com/if-youre-booking-event-speakers-dont-overlook-pre-event-calls)
- Washington Speakers Bureau lists common planner mistakes when booking speakers. — [WSB blog](https://blog.wsb.com/event-planner-mistakes-booking-speakers). Content not retrieved in detail.
- Controversial speakers: BizBash advises weighing whether the reward justifies the risk of conflict. Special Events recommends planning calls, on-site rehearsals and preparing the audience. BigSpeak markets vetting as protection against being "punked" (a reference to the Sarah Palin incident). — [BizBash](https://www.bizbash.com/production-strategy/programming-entertainment/article/22919644/how-to-manage-a-controversial-event-speaker); [Special Events](https://www.specialevents.com/event-management/he-said-what-how-to-prepare-for-controversial-speakers); [BigSpeak 2018](https://www.bigspeak.com/we-wont-let-you-or-your-business-get-punked-like-palin/)
- No-shows: MPI describes a speaker's last-minute cancellation as a nightmare scenario for planners and gives ways to handle it. Speakers Inc. advises contract terms requiring reimbursement when a cancellation is not force majeure. — [MPI blog](https://media.mpi.org/blog/article/4-ways-to-handle-event-speaker-no-shows); [Speakers Inc.](https://speakersinc.com/articles/hard-lessons-from-a-speaker-no-show)
- An event risk-management guide recommends a 4:1 speaker pipeline during booking (four prospects per slot) and a named, pre-approved backup for each keynote. — [webMOBI guide](https://www.webmobi.com/guides/event-marketing/event-risk-management)
- One advisor notes many planners mistake a signed contract for a completed task, which leaves risk unmanaged between booking and the event. — [speakers.com "How to Avoid a Keynote Speaker Disaster"](https://speakers.com/how-to-avoid-a-keynote-speaker-disaster-the-professional-planners-guide/)
- Unverified statistics to avoid: one speaker blog claims thorough vetting reduces speaker issues by up to 50% without naming a study. Another attributes an 85% figure to Event Manager Blog on a page with visible drafting placeholders. — [speakers.com](https://speakers.com/how-to-avoid-a-keynote-speaker-disaster-the-professional-planners-guide/); [Ian Khan checklist](https://www.iankhan.com/checklist-how-to-choose-the-right-keynote-speaker-for-your-event-3)

### Inferences
- Bureaus present themselves as handling the two biggest fears, fit/customization and cancellation. A matching platform would need equivalents: a structured pre-call or brief, a commitment to customize, and backup or replacement guarantees.
- Customization lead times (60–90 days ideal versus 4–6 weeks typical) point to a recurring source of generic content. A platform could surface lead-time requirements per instructor.

### Gaps
- No independent, quantified survey (from MPI, PCMA or Bizzabo) found on speaker no-show rates, controversy incidents, or how often planners use demo reels and reference checks.
- Demo reels and reference checks are commonly recommended in bureau content, but this pass found no data on how often planners use them.

## Q4. Trust and quality signals on two-sided service marketplaces (relevant to an instructor-matching platform)

### Takeaway
Field experiments on online labor markets show a strong cold-start problem. Employers undervalue untested workers, and the first job plus detailed public feedback sharply improves later outcomes (Pallais 2014). Intermediary affiliation (Stanton & Thomas 2016) and verified work history (Agrawal et al. 2016) substitute for missing reputation, most of all for newcomers and for groups facing statistical discrimination. Reputation also works on the demand side (Benson et al. 2020). But ratings inflate over time and lose information (Filippas, Horton & Golden 2022), and one bad transaction can damage trust in the whole platform (Nosko & Tadelis).

### Cited Findings
- Pallais (2014), "Inefficient Hiring in Entry-Level Labor Markets," *American Economic Review* 104(11):3565–3599. Field experiment on oDesk (now Upwork). Low-wage data-entry applicants were invited to 10-hour jobs; 3,767 who bid $3/hour or less formed the sample, and 952 were randomly hired and given detailed or coarse public evaluations. Being hired and receiving more detailed evaluations both substantially improved later employment and earnings. Under plausible assumptions, market-level benefits exceeded costs, which implies some workers were inefficiently unemployed because no one would hire them first. — [DOI 10.1257/aer.104.11.3565](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.104.11.3565); [NBER w18917](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18917); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:104:y:2014:i:11:p:3565-99)
- Stanton & Thomas (2016), "Landing the First Job: The Value of Intermediaries in Online Hiring," *Review of Economic Studies* 83(2):810–854. On oDesk, newcomers affiliated with outsourcing agencies had substantially higher job-finding probabilities and wages early in their careers than similar unaffiliated workers. The advantage fades once high-quality unaffiliated workers receive good public feedback. Intermediaries arose endogenously as a quality signal. — [DOI 10.1093/restud/rdv042](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdv042); [LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/65160)
- Agrawal, Lacetera & Lyons (2016), *Journal of International Economics* 103:1–12 (NBER w18720). On a large online contract labor platform, contractors from less developed countries were less likely to be hired. Verified work-experience information raised hiring for everyone but much more for those contractors (also seen in interviews and shortlisting). The premium was larger for experienced employers. A platform monitoring tool partly substituted for verified experience. — [DOI 10.1016/j.jinteco.2016.08.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jinteco.2016.08.003); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:inecon:v:103:y:2016:i:c:p:1-12)
- Benson, Sojourner & Umyarov (2020), "Can Reputation Discipline the Gig Economy?" *Management Science* 66(5):1802–1825. On Amazon MTurk, an audit study found that working only for good-reputation employers yields 40% higher wages. In an experiment, good-reputation employers attracted work of the same quality at twice the rate of bad-reputation employers. Small, good-reputation employers depend most on the reputation system to compete with established ones. — [DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2019.3303](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2019.3303)
- Filippas, Horton & Golden (2022), "Reputation Inflation," *Marketing Science* 41(4):733–745. Average ratings rose substantially over time across five online marketplaces. In the one market with detailed transaction data (an online labor market), much of the increase is reputation inflation, not better performance. The mechanism is raters' reluctance to harm sellers. The systems become less informative over time. — [DOI 10.1287/mksc.2022.1350](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2022.1350); [EurekAlert summary](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/954537); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.13028v1). The DOI suffix is from background knowledge; RePEc confirms vol. 41(4):733–745 [here](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:41:y:2022:i:4:p:733-745).
- Tadelis (2016), "Reputation and Feedback Systems in Online Platform Markets," *Annual Review of Economics* 8:321–340. Reviews how feedback systems work, the empirical evidence on them, and their shortcomings, including bias. — [DOI 10.1146/annurev-economics-080315-015325](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-economics-080315-015325)
- Nosko & Tadelis (NBER w20830), "The Limits of Reputation in Platform Markets." Buyers draw conclusions about the whole platform from single transactions, which creates a reputational externality across sellers. Platforms therefore have reason to control quality beyond showing ratings. — [NBER w20830](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20830)
- Ahmed, Tirmazee & Wu (IGC policy brief, Sept 2025) studied whether inexperienced freelancers can overcome cold start by offering lower introductory wages. Findings were not retrieved in detail. — [IGC brief](https://www.theigc.org/sites/default/files/2025-10/Ahmed-Tirmazee-Wu-Policy-Brief-September-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- For an instructor-matching platform, the cold-start evidence suggests new instructors with no ratings will be under-hired even when good. Platform-issued signals can fill the gap: verified credentials or work history, platform vetting or "affiliation" badges, structured detailed reviews, and subsidized or guaranteed first bookings. Pallais's and Stanton & Thomas's results suggest these help new entrants most.
- Because star ratings inflate, richer signals may stay more informative: detailed or structured reviews (Pallais found detailed evaluations helped more), repeat-booking rate and verified outcomes.
- Nosko & Tadelis's externality result implies that one bad instructor experience harms the platform's brand. Platform-level guarantees (replacement or refund) and active quality control are justified, mirroring how speaker bureaus guarantee backups.
- Benson et al. suggest two-sided reputation (rating client organizations too) could help attract quality instructors, especially for small client organizations.

### Gaps
- No study found that directly measures the effect of "verification badges," "money-back guarantees" or "rebooking rate" displays on hiring trainers or speakers specifically. The evidence comes from general online labor markets (oDesk/Upwork, MTurk).
- No academic study found on marketplaces specifically for corporate trainers or speakers.
